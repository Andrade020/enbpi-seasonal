\section{Simulation Study}
\label{sec:simulations}

We assess the finite-sample behaviour of \texttt{EnbPI-S} through
a controlled Monte Carlo study.  The study has five components:
E1 examines per-season coverage and interval width as a function
of training size; E2 isolates the effect of the per-stratum sample
size $T_s = T/\Sper$; E3 varies the heteroskedasticity intensity
$\sigma_{\mathrm{high}}$; E4 verifies behaviour in the null
(homoskedastic) case; and E5 checks robustness across two
different base learners.  All simulation code is available at
\texttt{code/simulation.py}.

%------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:simulations:setup}
%------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Data-generating process.}
We simulate from a seasonal autoregressive model with
heteroskedastic innovations:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:dgp_sim}
  Y_t \;=\; 0.6\,Y_{t-12} + 0.3\,Y_{t-1}
       + \sigma_{s(t)}\,\eta_t,
  \qquad
  \eta_t \;\stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\; t_3,
\end{equation}
where $t_3$ denotes the Student $t$-distribution with 3 degrees
of freedom.  The seasonal standard deviations are
\[
  \sigma_s \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    \sigma_{\mathrm{high}} & \text{if } s \in \{1,2,8,9\}, \\
    1                       & \text{otherwise},
  \end{cases}
\]
with default $\sigma_{\mathrm{high}} = 2$.  The high-volatility
seasons (January, February, August, September) mimic the IPCA
pattern studied in Section~\ref{sec:empirical}, where inflation
surprises are largest at the start of the year and in the
pre-harvest months.  The $t_3$ innovations introduce heavy tails,
making the calibration problem harder than under Gaussianity.
Each simulated series begins with a 60-period burn-in that is
discarded.

\paragraph{Features and base learner.}
For each observation at time $t$, the feature vector consists of
the $p = 13$ most recent lags: $x_t = (Y_{t-1}, \ldots, Y_{t-13})$.
The default base learner $\calA$ is Ridge regression
($\ell_2$-penalty $= 1.0$); Experiment E5 also uses a Random
Forest ($B_{\mathrm{tree}} = 30$ trees, maximum depth 6).
Both methods use $B = 50$ bootstrap models, batch size
$s_0 = 1$, and significance level $\alpha = 0.10$.

\paragraph{Methods compared.}
Experiments E1--E4 compare three methods:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item \emph{SARIMA-Gaussian}: a seasonal ARIMA$(1,0,0)(1,0,0)_{12}$
    model fitted by Gaussian MLE, with symmetric prediction intervals
    $\hat{f}_t \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\hat{\sigma}$,
    where $\hat{\sigma}$ is the in-sample residual standard deviation.
    This is the standard parametric baseline used in applied
    forecasting.
  \item \emph{Pooled EnbPI}: the original algorithm of
    \citet{xu2023conformal}, which maintains a single calibration
    buffer of $T - p$ LOO residuals and uses the distribution-free
    line search of Section~\ref{sec:model:enbpi}.
  \item \emph{\texttt{EnbPI-S}}: Algorithm~\ref{alg:enbpis},
    which maintains $\Sper = 12$ season-specific calibration buffers.
\end{enumerate}
Methods (ii) and (iii) use the same Ridge bootstrap ensemble,
so the only difference between them is in calibration.
Method (i) differs in both the forecast model (SARIMA vs ensemble)
and the interval construction (parametric Gaussian vs empirical quantile).
Experiment E5 (predictor robustness) uses only methods (ii) and (iii).

\paragraph{Evaluation metrics.}
For each experiment we record, for each of the $\Sper = 12$
seasons:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item \emph{Empirical coverage}: fraction of $T_1$ test
    observations in season $s$ whose true value lies within the
    predicted interval;
  \item \emph{Average width}: mean length of the predicted
    intervals over test observations in season $s$.
\end{enumerate}
We also record overall (season-averaged) coverage and width.
Unless stated otherwise, $T_1 = 120$ (ten years) test
observations are used.  Monte Carlo standard errors are
available in the replication archive (\texttt{code/simulation.py}).

\subsection{E1: Per-Season Coverage and Width}
\label{sec:simulations:e1}

The two-method table (Pooled vs \texttt{EnbPI-S} only) is
reproduced in Appendix~\ref{app:additional}
(Table~\ref{tab:e1_coverage_T480}) for reference.

\paragraph{Motivating the benchmark.}
SARIMA$(1,0,0)(1,0,0)_{12}$ is the natural parametric competitor:
it is correctly specified for the seasonal mean structure of
the DGP~\eqref{eq:dgp_sim}, so any failure in its prediction
intervals is attributable entirely to distributional or variance
misspecification, not to a poor point forecast.
This makes SARIMA-Gaussian an informative strawman that isolates
the two ingredients our paper addresses:
non-Gaussianity of the error distribution ($t_3$ vs $\mathcal{N}$)
and seasonal heteroskedasticity of its variance ($\sigma_s \neq
\mathrm{const}$).

\paragraph{Coverage uniformity.}
The pooled method exhibits severe seasonal bias: its coverage
ranges from approximately $0.78$ for the high-volatility months
(January, February, August, September, marked with *) to $0.96$
for the low-volatility months — a range of roughly
$0.18$ probability units.  The stratified method reduces this
spread dramatically: all twelve months lie within a
$0.02$-unit band, between approximately $0.83$ and $0.85$.
This confirms the theoretical prediction of
Proposition~\ref{prop:pooled_gap}: pooled calibration induces
a persistent, season-dependent bias of order $2b_{s^*}$,
whereas stratified calibration removes it.

\paragraph{Width adaptation.}
A complementary view comes from the width column.
The pooled method produces a nearly constant interval width
of $\approx 6.38$ across all seasons.
By contrast, \texttt{EnbPI-S} widens its intervals by a factor
of $\approx 1.28$ for high-volatility seasons (width $\approx 8.2$)
and narrows them by a factor of $\approx 0.66$ for
low-volatility seasons (width $\approx 4.2$).
The ratio of mean high-volatility width to mean low-volatility
width for \texttt{EnbPI-S} is approximately $8.2/4.2 \approx 1.95$,
closely tracking the true ratio $\sigma_{\mathrm{high}}/\sigma_{\mathrm{low}} = 2/1 = 2$.
This shows that \texttt{EnbPI-S} correctly recovers the
seasonal volatility structure from the calibration buffers.

\paragraph{SARIMA-Gaussian: two compounding failures.}
The SARIMA benchmark reveals why a standard parametric approach
is doubly inadequate.  First, Gaussian prediction intervals
underestimate the heavy tails of the $t_3$ error distribution.
Second, the Gaussian MLE estimates a single $\hat{\sigma}$
from the pooled residuals, which is too large for
low-volatility months (causing overcoverage $\approx 0.87$)
and too small for high-volatility months
(causing severe undercoverage: mean $0.699$, worst $0.662$
for September).
The resulting coverage spread ($0.243$ probability units) is
$36\%$ wider than that of Pooled EnbPI ($0.179$) and
more than twelve times that of \texttt{EnbPI-S} ($0.019$).
For September specifically, SARIMA achieves only $66.2\%$
coverage --- more than $23$ percentage points below nominal ---
while \texttt{EnbPI-S} achieves $84.4\%$.

The comparison isolates the two contributions of this paper:
Pooled EnbPI corrects SARIMA's distributional assumption
through empirical quantiles, improving high-volatility
coverage from $0.699$ to $0.786$ ($+8.7$ pp);
\texttt{EnbPI-S} further corrects the seasonal calibration
bias, raising it to $0.842$ ($+5.6$ pp more) and
collapsing the coverage spread to $0.019$.

\paragraph{Coverage accuracy for high-volatility seasons.}
Among the three methods, \texttt{EnbPI-S} is uniquely capable
of achieving near-uniform coverage.
For the four high-volatility months (*),
SARIMA undercovers by $20$ percentage points (mean gap),
Pooled EnbPI by $11$--$12$ percentage points,
and \texttt{EnbPI-S} by only $5$--$6$ percentage points.
September illustrates the contrast starkly:
SARIMA $0.662$, Pooled $0.776$, \texttt{EnbPI-S} $0.844$.

\paragraph{Worst-case coverage.}
From a risk-management perspective the relevant quantity is the
\emph{minimum} per-season coverage.
SARIMA achieves a worst-case coverage of $0.662$
(September); Pooled EnbPI $0.776$; \texttt{EnbPI-S}
raises this floor to $0.844$.
The ordering is unambiguous: \texttt{EnbPI-S} provides the
strongest guarantee for the seasons that matter most.

\paragraph{Effect of training size.}
Table~\ref{tab:e1_coverage_T240} (Appendix~\ref{app:additional})
repeats the analysis for $T = 240$ (20 years).  The qualitative
pattern is identical: \texttt{EnbPI-S} reduces seasonal
coverage dispersion and narrows intervals in low-volatility
months.  The absolute coverage values are lower for both methods
at $T = 240$ due to the reduced per-stratum sample size
$T_s \approx 20$; this finite-sample effect is studied
in Experiment E2 below.

%------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{E2: Effect of Stratum Size}
\label{sec:simulations:e2}
%------------------------------------------------------------

Theorem~\ref{thm:strat_coverage} predicts that the stratified
coverage bound converges to zero at rate $O(\sqrt{\log T_s / T_s})$,
where $T_s = T/\Sper$ is the per-stratum sample size.
Experiment E2 isolates this effect by varying $T_s \in
\{5, 10, 20, 30, 50\}$ (corresponding to $T \in \{60, 120, 240,
360, 600\}$ months), holding $T_1 = 120$ and
$\sigma_{\mathrm{high}} = 2$ fixed.

\input{tables/tab_E2}

The results, shown in Table~\ref{tab:e2_stratum_size}, reveal
two key phenomena.  First, for very small $T_s$ (e.g.\ $T_s = 5$),
\texttt{EnbPI-S} falls well short of nominal coverage ($\approx 0.52$),
while pooled EnbPI achieves $\approx 0.84$.  This is a genuine
finite-sample limitation: with fewer than ten residuals per
season, the per-stratum empirical quantiles are too noisy to
serve as reliable calibration points.  Second, as $T_s$ grows,
the stratified coverage improves monotonically from $0.50$
at $T_s = 5$ to $0.84$ at $T_s = 50$, consistent with the
$O(\sqrt{\log T_s / T_s})$ decay in the theoretical bound.
The pooled coverage, by contrast, plateaus near $0.88$ regardless
of $T$: the persistent bias $2b_{s^*}$ prevents it from
converging to the nominal level.

\begin{remark}[Practical sample-size guidance]
\label{rem:min_ts}
The results suggest that \texttt{EnbPI-S} requires at least
$T_s \approx 30$--$40$ observations per season to be competitive
with pooled EnbPI.  For monthly data with $\Sper = 12$, this
corresponds to $T \approx 360$--$480$ months ($30$--$40$ years).
The food-IPCA application (Section~\ref{sec:empirical:food})
uses $T = 240$ months ($\Ts = 20$), below this range, which
is consistent with the coverage shortfall observed there.
The export growth application (Section~\ref{sec:empirical:exports})
uses $T = 430$ months ($\Ts \approx 35$), falling within the
recommended range and achieving coverage within $3.3$ percentage
points of nominal.
\end{remark}

%------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{E3: Heteroskedasticity Intensity}
\label{sec:simulations:e3}
%------------------------------------------------------------

The benefit of stratification grows with the degree of
heteroskedasticity.  We vary $\sigma_{\mathrm{high}} \in
\{1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 3.0\}$ with $T = 240$ fixed.

When $\sigma_{\mathrm{high}} = 1$ (null case, identical to E4
below), both methods perform similarly and the advantage of
\texttt{EnbPI-S} is small, limited to coverage uniformity.
As $\sigma_{\mathrm{high}}$ increases, the pooled coverage for
high-volatility seasons deteriorates markedly:
at $\sigma_{\mathrm{high}} = 3$, pooled coverage in January
falls to $\approx 0.74$, while the stratified method maintains
coverage near $0.78$--$0.81$ for the same seasons.
Simultaneously, the pooled coverage for low-volatility months
rises to $\approx 0.95$--$0.97$, reflecting increasingly
inflated intervals.  \texttt{EnbPI-S} keeps both high- and
low-volatility months near the same (if imperfect) coverage
level, demonstrating robust adaptation to the unknown
heteroskedasticity intensity.

The width ratio EnbPI-S\,/\,Pooled for high-volatility seasons
tracks $\sigma_{\mathrm{high}}$: at $\sigma_{\mathrm{high}} = 3$,
\texttt{EnbPI-S} produces intervals approximately $1.4\times$
wider than Pooled for January and February, consistently with
the inflated residual scale for those months.

%------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{E4: Null Case}
\label{sec:simulations:e4}
%------------------------------------------------------------

To quantify the cost of stratification in the absence of
heteroskedasticity, we set $\sigma_s = 1$ for all $s$ with
$T = 240$.  In this case the pooled and stratified calibration
buffers are calibrated on residuals from the same distribution,
and the theoretical bias $b_{s^*} = 0$.

Empirically, pooled EnbPI achieves overall coverage of
$\approx 0.88$, close to the nominal $0.90$, with small
variation across seasons ($\approx 0.86$--$0.90$).
\texttt{EnbPI-S} achieves only $\approx 0.80$ overall,
a gap of roughly eight percentage points.
This gap represents the \emph{stratification cost}: using
only $T_s \approx 19$ residuals per season instead of the full
$T - p \approx 227$ enlarges the finite-sample error of the
empirical quantile.

This result has an important practical implication: an analyst
who is uncertain whether seasonal heteroskedasticity is present
should prefer \texttt{EnbPI-S} only if there is enough data
(see Remark~\ref{rem:min_ts}) and if the heteroskedasticity
is sufficiently pronounced to outweigh the stratification cost.
The dominance condition (D) in
Proposition~\ref{prop:strat_dominance} makes this trade-off
precise in terms of $b_{s^*}$, $T$, and $S$.

%------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{E5: Predictor Robustness}
\label{sec:simulations:e5}
%------------------------------------------------------------

The seasonal bias identified in Proposition~\ref{prop:pooled_gap}
is a property of the calibration procedure, not the point
forecast $\hat{f}$.  We verify this by repeating the main
experiment with $T = 240$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{high}} = 2$
using two base learners: Ridge regression and a Random Forest.
In both cases, the qualitative pattern is preserved: pooled
EnbPI overcovers low-volatility and undercovers
high-volatility seasons, while \texttt{EnbPI-S} reduces this
dispersion.  The magnitude of the effect is nearly identical
across learners, confirming that the gain from stratification
is \emph{model-agnostic}: it depends only on the seasonal
structure of the residual distribution, not on the quality of
the point forecast.

The three-method comparison tells a progressive story.
SARIMA-Gaussian fails on both axes: it underestimates heavy
tails and ignores seasonal variance differences, producing
a worst-case coverage of $0.662$ in September
(Table~\ref{tab:e1_three_methods_T480}).
Pooled EnbPI corrects the distributional assumption through
empirical quantiles, improving worst-case coverage to $0.776$,
but retains the seasonal bias of pooled calibration.
\texttt{EnbPI-S} corrects both, raising worst-case coverage
to $0.844$ and reducing the coverage spread from $0.243$
(SARIMA) and $0.179$ (Pooled) to $0.019$ ---
more than a twelve-fold reduction relative to SARIMA.

The main message is that \texttt{EnbPI-S} provides more
\emph{balanced} and \emph{efficient} prediction intervals
whenever seasonal heteroskedasticity is present and the
per-stratum sample size $T_s$ is at least $30$--$40$.
Its principal cost — lower coverage in the null case —
diminishes as $T$ grows and is absent when the analyst
correctly identifies the seasonal structure.
The empirical application in Section~\ref{sec:empirical}
illustrates these trade-offs on real Brazilian inflation data.
