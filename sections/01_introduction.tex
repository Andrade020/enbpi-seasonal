\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Reliable prediction intervals are indispensable whenever point
forecasts are deployed in high-stakes settings: monetary policy
decisions hinge on the uncertainty around inflation projections,
energy-market participants require interval estimates that correctly
reflect demand volatility in winter as opposed to summer, and
supply-chain planners need intervals that are not systematically too
narrow in peak months.  In all these settings the decisive feature is
\emph{seasonal heteroskedasticity}, that is, the variability of
forecast errors differs systematically by calendar month or
quarter.\footnote{Throughout the paper the word ``season'' denotes a
position within a periodic cycle of known length $S$ (a calendar
month when $S = 12$, a quarter when $S = 4$), and not the
meteorological season.}

The conformal-prediction literature has made substantial progress in
constructing prediction intervals for time series
\citep{xu2023conformal, xu2023sequential, barber2023conformal},
though the problem of \emph{seasonal} heteroskedasticity has received
comparatively little attention.  The standard approach, which we call
\emph{pooled calibration}, calibrates intervals on a single buffer of
residuals drawn from all seasons indiscriminately.  When the
distribution of forecast errors varies by season, pooled calibration
introduces a persistent, season-dependent bias, so that intervals
turn out too narrow in volatile seasons (undercoverage) and too wide
in stable ones (overcoverage), no matter how large the training set
becomes.

This paper makes three contributions.  The first is a formalisation
of the seasonal bias: we derive an explicit upper bound for the
per-season coverage gap of the pooled EnbPI algorithm
\citep{xu2023conformal}, and the bound separates into a vanishing
sampling term and a non-vanishing bias term $2b_{s^*}$, where
$b_{s^*} = \sup_x |F_{s^*}(x) - \bar{F}(x)|$ measures the CDF
discrepancy between season $s^*$ and the pooled residual
distribution (Proposition~\ref{prop:pooled_gap}).
The second is the algorithm itself.  \texttt{EnbPI-S}
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:enbpis}) is a stratified extension of EnbPI that
maintains $\Sper$ separate calibration buffers, one per calendar
season, and its only departure from EnbPI lies in the calibration
step: the line search for the optimal asymmetric quantile pair uses
only residuals drawn from the same season as the prediction target.
The training phase is unchanged, no data splitting is required, and
the total memory footprint is identical to that of EnbPI.
The third contribution is theoretical.  We prove that the per-season
coverage gap of \texttt{EnbPI-S} converges to zero at rate
$O(\sqrt{\log T_s / T_s})$, where $T_s = T/\Sper$ is the per-stratum
training size (Theorem~\ref{thm:strat_coverage}); we derive an
asymptotic dominance result (Proposition~\ref{prop:strat_dominance});
and we bound the width gap, showing that the \texttt{EnbPI-S}
interval converges to the season-specific oracle interval
(Theorem~\ref{thm:width_bound}).  A finite-sample counterpart of the
dominance result is also recorded
\eqref{eq:dominance_condition}, though
Remark~\ref{rem:dominance_practical} shows that worst-case bounds of
this type are too loose to be checked in an application, so the
finite-sample comparison in this paper rests on simulation rather
than on the bounds.

A fourth element is less a contribution than a boundary, and the
paper is explicit about it.  Stratification is not the only way to
undo the pooled bias.  If the seasonal effect is a pure scale effect,
dividing each residual by a season-specific scale estimate and
calibrating on the pooled standardised buffer removes the same bias
while spending only $\Sper$ parameters on the seasonal structure,
which is the seasonal counterpart of normalised conformal prediction
\citep{dewolf2025conditional}.  Our simulations show that this
competitor, denoted \texttt{EnbPI-N}, is the more efficient of the two
whenever the scale restriction holds, since it estimates the shape of
the residual distribution from the whole sample: at twenty observations
per season its interval is a fifth narrower than the stratified one at
comparable coverage.  Which of the two lands closer to the nominal
level depends on how the interval is built, and that turns out to
matter more than the choice between them.  The case for stratification is that it does
not require the restriction: when seasons differ in the shape of the
error distribution and not only in its scale, a scale correction has
nothing to correct, and in that configuration \texttt{EnbPI-S} cuts
the coverage spread by two thirds while normalisation leaves it
essentially where pooling left it.  Which configuration holds is
testable, and on both Brazilian series studied here a permutation
test rejects the pure-scale restriction.

The paper's fifth element was not planned, and it is the one we would
now put first.  Stratification is generally held to be too expensive at
the per-season sample sizes a monthly series provides, and the earlier
sections of this paper measure that expense at about eleven percentage
points of coverage at twenty residuals per season.  Most of it turns
out to belong to the interval construction rather than to the
conditioning.  The empirical quantile of a short buffer is biased
inwards, and the minimum-width line search returns the narrowest of
many candidate quantile pairs computed from the same residuals, which
by itself costs between four and six percentage points at these buffer
lengths.  Reading the buffer as the conformal order statistic it is
meant to be, with the asymmetry fixed at $\alpha/2$, repairs both, and
stratified coverage at twenty residuals per season then reaches its
nominal level in all four seasonal designs we study and on both
applications.  What remains is a cost in width, which is the honest
price of a finite-sample guarantee on a short buffer, and two exact
thresholds govern it: the conformal indices exist only for
$\Ts \geq 2/\alpha - 1$ and fall strictly inside the sample only for
$\Ts \geq 4/\alpha - 1$, that is $19$ and $39$ observations per season
at the $90\%$ level.

Two empirical applications illustrate the argument, both of them run
over the test period January 2015 to December 2024.  In the first,
food-at-home IPCA (BCB series 1635, $T_s = 20$), \texttt{EnbPI-S}
displays clear width adaptation, with a width ratio of $1.78\times$
between the most and the least uncertain months, consistent with the
data-limited predictions of Experiment E2.  In the second, Brazilian
export growth (BCB series 1402, $T_s = 35$), both methods achieve
coverage within $3.3$ percentage points of nominal, on opposite
sides, and the width ratio rises to $2.3\times$: \texttt{EnbPI-S}
assigns a $13.8\%$ interval to April, the peak of the soybean
harvest-export season and the month of highest forecast risk, and a
$6.0\%$ interval to September, the most predictable month, whereas
the pooled method applies a constant $10.2\%$ throughout.

Conformal prediction for time series has been developed along several
orthogonal dimensions.  \citet{xu2023conformal} introduced EnbPI,
which handles non-exchangeability through a leave-one-out (LOO)
bootstrap ensemble and a sliding-window update.
\citet{xu2023sequential} extended it to exploit residual
autocorrelation (Sequential Predictive Conformal Inference, SPCI),
improving efficiency under serial dependence.
\citet{barber2023conformal} established coverage guarantees for
non-exchangeable data through a weighted conformal framework.
On the conditional validity side, the Mondrian conformal prediction
of \citet{vovk2005mondrian} achieves category-conditional coverage
for i.i.d.\ data by stratifying the calibration set, and
\citet{dewolf2025conditional} show that normalised conformal
prediction achieves conditional validity for i.i.d.\ data from a
location-scale family without any stratification.

\texttt{EnbPI-S} bridges these streams, since it applies
Mondrian-style stratification to the non-exchangeable, temporally
dependent setting of EnbPI, and thereby delivers asymptotically
valid season-conditional intervals without requiring i.i.d.\ data,
data splitting, or parametric distributional assumptions.  Our
approach is complementary to SPCI: SPCI corrects for residual
autocorrelation, while \texttt{EnbPI-S} corrects for seasonal
heteroskedasticity, and the two can in principle be combined
(Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}).

The simulation study also benchmarks \texttt{EnbPI-S} against a
seasonal autoregressive integrated moving average model,
SARIMA$(1,0,0)(1,0,0)_{12}$, with Gaussian prediction intervals,
which is the standard parametric alternative.  The SARIMA mean
structure closely approximates that of our data-generating process
(DGP; see Section~\ref{sec:simulations}), so that its failure, a
September coverage of $66.2\%$ against the $90\%$ nominal, is
attributable almost entirely to two compounding distributional
misspecifications: Gaussian tails, which underestimate the heavy
$t_3$ innovations, and a single pooled $\hat{\sigma}$, which ignores
seasonal variance heterogeneity.  Pooled EnbPI corrects the first
failure through empirical quantiles ($+8.8$ pp on high-volatility
coverage); \texttt{EnbPI-S} corrects both ($+5.5$ pp further),
reducing the cross-season coverage spread more than twelve-fold
relative to SARIMA ($0.019$ against $0.243$).

As to notation, $\Sper$ denotes the number of seasons, $T$ the
training size, $T_s = T/\Sper$ the per-stratum size,
$\alpha \in (0,1)$ the miscoverage level, and $s(t)$ the season of
time $t$.  Empirical quantiles are written
$Q_p(\hat{\boldsymbol\epsilon})$ for the $p$-th quantile of the
multiset $\hat{\boldsymbol\epsilon}$.  All other notation is defined
where it is introduced.
