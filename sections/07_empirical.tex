\section{Empirical Applications}
\label{sec:empirical}

Two empirical applications are presented.  The first
(Section~\ref{sec:empirical:food}) uses the food-at-home component of
IPCA (1995--2024, $T_s = 20$ per season) to illustrate
\texttt{EnbPI-S} in a data-limited regime typical of Brazilian price
statistics.  The second (Section~\ref{sec:empirical:exports}) uses
Brazilian monthly export growth (1979--2024, $T_s = 35$ per season)
to validate the method in the sample-size regime where
Theorem~\ref{thm:strat_coverage} and Experiment E2 predict reliable
coverage improvements.  Both applications use the test period
January 2015 to December 2024, which enables a direct
cross-application comparison.

%------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Food-at-Home IPCA (\texorpdfstring{$T_s = 20$}{Ts = 20})}
\label{sec:empirical:food}
%------------------------------------------------------------

The first illustration concerns the forecasting of the food-at-home
component of Brazilian consumer inflation
(IPCA\,--\,Alimentação no domicílio), a seasonally heteroskedastic
monthly series that provides a cleaner test case than headline
IPCA.\footnote{``Alimentação no domicílio'' designates food
purchased for consumption at home, as opposed to ``Alimentação fora
do domicílio'', food away from home; the two are the subgroups into
which the IPCA food and beverages group is divided, and only the
former is used here (our translation).}
Monthly percentage changes in that component, compiled by IBGE
\citep{ibge2024ipca}, are downloaded from the Banco Central do Brasil
(BCB) open-data API \citep{bcb2024sgs}, Sistema Gerenciador de
S\'eries (SGS) series 1635.  The series is available from January
1994 at monthly frequency, and observations from January 1995 onwards
are used here.

The food component exhibits substantially stronger seasonal
heteroskedasticity than headline IPCA, for two reasons: agricultural
prices follow harvest cycles, with residual volatility systematically
higher in the pre-harvest and post-harvest adjustment months, May and
June in Brazil, and the food component is less contaminated by
administered-price shocks, such as fuel and electricity, which
dominate headline IPCA in some years and whose seasonality differs
from the food cycle.  Both features make food IPCA a sharper test for
seasonal calibration.

The sample is split as follows.  Training runs from January 1995 to
December 2014 ($T = 240$ months, 20 years, $T_s = 20$ observations
per season), and the test period runs from January 2015 to December
2024 ($T_1 = 120$ months, $n_s = 10$ observations per
season).\footnote{The $p = 13$ autoregressive lags consume the first
year of the sample, so each seasonal calibration buffer effectively
holds $T_s - 1 \approx 19$ LOO residuals; we quote the nominal $T_s$
throughout.}
Feature vector, bootstrap ensemble and significance level are
identical to the simulation setup: $p = 13$ AR lags, $B = 50$ Ridge
models, $\alpha = 0.10$.
Table~\ref{tab:empirical_food_coverage} reports per-season coverage
and average interval width over the test period.

\input{tables/tab_empirical_food_coverage}

The central empirical finding is that \texttt{EnbPI-S} recovers the
seasonal structure of forecast uncertainty.  The pooled method
produces an essentially constant interval width of $\approx 1.82$
percentage points across all twelve months, blind to seasonal
variations in predictability, whereas \texttt{EnbPI-S} produces
widths that range from $1.05$ p.p.\ in February, historically stable
in the post-January reset, to $1.86$ p.p.\ in June, the mid-year
food-price adjustment period in Brazil, a factor-of-$1.78$ variation.
The months with the widest \texttt{EnbPI-S} intervals (June, May,
November) correspond to periods of greater harvest-price uncertainty,
and those with the narrowest intervals (February, April, October)
correspond to more predictable stretches of the food-price cycle.

Figure~\ref{fig:empirical_food_width_time} illustrates this
contrast: the Pooled band (red) is nearly flat throughout the decade,
while the \texttt{EnbPI-S} band (blue) visibly pulses with the
food-price seasonal calendar, widening in May and June and narrowing
in February and October.  The bottom panel confirms the pattern
across all twelve months, with the pooled bars essentially uniform
and the \texttt{EnbPI-S} bars tracking the seasonal uncertainty
rhythm of Brazilian food prices.

Overall empirical coverage is $85.8\%$ for Pooled and $79.2\%$ for
\texttt{EnbPI-S} against the $90\%$ nominal.  Both methods fall short
of nominal, in line with the finite-sample prediction of
Experiment E2: at $T_s = 20$ observations per stratum, the simulation
found Pooled achieving $\approx 88\%$ and \texttt{EnbPI-S}
$\approx 78\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:e2_stratum_size}).  The empirical
figures, $85.8\%$ and $79.2\%$, match that pattern closely, which
confirms that the simulation reproduces what the algorithm does on
data.  Section~\ref{sec:empirical:choice} revisits both figures: the
shortfall is a property of the interval construction rather than of
the stratification, and the same buffer read differently covers at the
nominal level.  Per-season
coverage detail is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:coverage_by_month}
(Section~\ref{sec:empirical:choice}); with only $n_s = 10$ observations
per season, individual points carry standard errors of $\approx 0.14$,
so that month-level comparisons should not be over-interpreted.

The width pattern produced by \texttt{EnbPI-S} is economically
meaningful.  Brazil's food-price calendar exhibits a well-documented
seasonal cycle: harvest periods, March and April for grains, May and
June for winter vegetables and milk, produce volatile price
adjustments as supply conditions are realised, while February is
characterised by stable demand following the post-Carnival slowdown.
\texttt{EnbPI-S} internalises this calendar from the calibration
residuals, widening intervals automatically when forecasting
uncertainty is high, without requiring explicit seasonal dummy
variables or prior knowledge of the harvest calendar.  The pooled
method, working from a single calibration buffer, cannot make this
distinction and applies a uniform bandwidth throughout the year.

The empirical results connect to the simulation study in two concrete
ways.  First, the ratio of maximum to minimum \texttt{EnbPI-S}
interval width, June to February, $1.864/1.046 \approx 1.78$,
reflects the moderate seasonal heteroskedasticity of Brazilian food
prices, and Theorem~\ref{thm:width_bound} guarantees that, as $T_s$
grows, each seasonal width converges to the corresponding
season-specific oracle width.  The pooled method's constant width of
$\approx 1.82$ p.p.\ lies between these extremes, consistent with
the bias identified in Proposition~\ref{prop:pooled_gap}: pooled
calibration overcovers the stable months and undercovers the volatile
ones.  Second, the coverage shortfall relative to the $90\%$ nominal
is the finite-sample penalty at $T_s = 20$ documented in Experiment
E2, a simulated gap of $11.5$ pp for \texttt{EnbPI-S} against an
empirical gap of $10.8$ pp here.
Section~\ref{sec:empirical:exports} below provides an application
with $T_s = 35$, confirming that the coverage gap shrinks
substantially as the stratum size increases.

%------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Brazilian Monthly Export Growth (\texorpdfstring{$T_s = 35$}{Ts = 35})}
\label{sec:empirical:exports}
%------------------------------------------------------------

Monthly values of Brazilian total exports (BCB SGS series 1402, in
US\$ millions; \citealp{bcb2024sgs}) are available from February
1979.  The series is transformed into log-returns,
$Y_t = 100 \cdot \log(V_t / V_{t-1})$, where $V_t$ denotes the
monthly export value, which yields a stationary series of monthly
export growth rates in per cent.  Since the series is denominated in
US dollars, it is unaffected by Brazilian hyperinflation, which
enables a training window of almost 36 years: training runs from
March 1979 to December 2014 ($T = 430$; each calendar month
contributes 35 or 36 observations, and we quote $T_s = 35$, the
minimum), and the test period runs from January 2015 to December 2024
($T_1 = 120$, the same window as in
Section~\ref{sec:empirical:food}).  Feature vector, ensemble and
significance level are identical to the previous application.

Brazilian export flows are dominated by agricultural commodities with
pronounced harvest cycles.  Soybean, the largest export by value, is
planted between October and January, harvested between February and
May, and shipped in bulk from March through June, while coffee and
beef follow different but equally seasonal rhythms.  These cycles
produce clear month-to-month differences in forecast-error variance:
April sits at the peak of the harvest-export ramp-up, when the size
of the new crop and the pace of its shipment are still being
revealed, whereas September, past the export peak and before the next
planting signals arrive, is the most predictable month.  This natural
experiment provides a strong test for seasonal interval calibration.
Table~\ref{tab:empirical_exports_coverage} reports per-season
coverage and width.

\input{tables/tab_empirical_exports_coverage}

With $T_s = 35$, both methods are substantially closer to the nominal
$90\%$ level than in the food-IPCA application ($T_s = 20$): the
pooled method achieves $93.3\%$ overall coverage, overcovering by
$3.3$ percentage points, and \texttt{EnbPI-S} achieves $86.7\%$,
undercovering by the same $3.3$ percentage points.  Both are
equidistant from the nominal level, in keeping with Experiment E2's
prediction that the coverage gap shrinks as the stratum size grows
(simulated \texttt{EnbPI-S} gaps of $11.5$, $7.8$ and $5.5$
percentage points at $T_s = 20$, $30$ and $50$); the empirical gap of
$3.3$ percentage points at $T_s = 35$ is in fact smaller than the
simulated trajectory suggests.

The width-adaptation finding is even more pronounced than in the
food-IPCA case.  The pooled method's intervals are essentially
constant at $\approx 10.2\%$ across all twelve months (ratio $1.01$),
whereas \texttt{EnbPI-S} produces intervals ranging from $6.0\%$ in
September, the most predictable month, to $13.8\%$ in April, the peak
of the harvest-export season, a factor-of-$2.3$ variation that
directly reflects the seasonal structure of forecast uncertainty.

April is the month in which the two methods differ most: pooled
coverage is $70\%$ (7 of 10 test observations covered) against $90\%$
for \texttt{EnbPI-S} (9 of 10), which assigns a wide interval
($13.8\%$) to accommodate the extreme export-growth swings associated
with the shipment of the new soybean crop, while the pooled interval
($10.2\%$) is too narrow for that month.  The gap rests on two
observations, so it should be read as an illustration of the
mechanism rather than as evidence in itself; the width assignment
behind it, estimated from $35$ April residuals, is the more
informative quantity.

Figure~\ref{fig:empirical_exports_width_time} illustrates the
contrast over the full test period: the Pooled band (red) is nearly
flat at $\approx 10.2\%$ throughout, while the \texttt{EnbPI-S} band
(blue) peaks in the second quarter, during the harvest-export
ramp-up, and troughs in the third quarter.  The bottom panel confirms
that \texttt{EnbPI-S} allocates maximum uncertainty to the months
from March to May, peaking in April, and minimum uncertainty to
September.

The pattern across applications is consistent with the simulation
findings.  As $T_s$ increases from 20 to 35, the \texttt{EnbPI-S}
coverage gap falls from $10.8\%$ to $3.3\%$, a reduction of more than
two-thirds, matching Experiment E2's qualitative prediction.  In both
applications the pooled method applies a nearly constant interval
width, while \texttt{EnbPI-S} identifies and adapts to the seasonal
structure of forecast uncertainty, a conclusion that is robust to the
choice of economic series.

\subsection{Normalise or Stratify?}
\label{sec:empirical:choice}
%------------------------------------------------------------

Experiment E6 established two things that the applications can test.
Stratification is not the only way to remove the pooled bias, and the
choice between normalising and stratifying turns on whether the seasonal
effect is a pure scale effect; and the coverage that stratified
calibration loses at short buffers is mostly recovered by building the
interval from conformal order statistics with a fixed asymmetry.

\input{tables/tab_empirical_family}

Table~\ref{tab:empirical_family} runs all eight schemes on the two
series, and the second point is the more striking of the two.  On the
food application, where each season contributes twenty training
observations and, after the autoregressive lags, nineteen usable
residuals, the stratified scheme as the original algorithm builds it
covers $79.2\%$ against a nominal $90\%$; the same buffer read with
conformal order statistics covers exactly $90.0\%$, and does so with a
seasonal spread of zero, every calendar month being covered in nine of
its ten test observations.  On the export application, at about
thirty-five residuals per season, the same change takes coverage from
$86.7\%$ to $95.0\%$ and the worst month from $70\%$ to $90\%$.  The
price is width, as the simulation says it must be: $2.26$ against $1.44$
percentage points for food, and $16.6$ against $8.5$ per cent for
exports, the latter being the conservatism of an interval that is still
the range of its buffer at $\Ts = 35$.  Blending towards the normalised
endpoint recovers part of it, taking the export width from $16.6$ to
$13.6$ at the same $95.0\%$ coverage.
Figure~\ref{fig:coverage_by_month} shows the shift month by month.

Whether normalisation would have been enough can be assessed directly
from the training residuals, and this is where the first point is
tested.  Let $\hat{b} = \max_s \sup_x |\hat{F}_s(x) -
\hat{F}^{\text{pool}}(x)|$ be the empirical counterpart of the seasonal
bias of Section~\ref{sec:pooled_gap:discussion}, and let $\hat{b}^{N}$ be
the same statistic computed on residuals standardised by their
season-specific scale, that is, the part of the seasonal discrepancy that
a scale correction cannot remove.  Under the hypothesis that season
labels carry no information about the residual distribution the labels
are exchangeable, so the distribution of both statistics is obtained by
permuting them; $999$ permutations give the reference, with the season
scales re-estimated inside each permutation so that the null is respected
in both cases.

For the food series, $\hat{b} = 0.380$ ($p = 0.042$) and
$\hat{b}^{N} = 0.428$ ($p = 0.052$); for exports, $\hat{b} = 0.307$
($p = 0.011$) and $\hat{b}^{N} = 0.324$ ($p = 0.008$).  The reading is
the same in both cases.  The seasonal discrepancy is real, which is what
makes pooled calibration inappropriate, and standardising by a
season-specific scale does not reduce it: what survives normalisation is
as large as what was there to begin with, and remains significant at
conventional levels for exports and borderline for food.  On these two
series seasonality is not a pure scale effect, which is the configuration
in which stratification is the remedy that works and normalisation is
not.

The weights chosen by cross-validation are consistent with that reading
without being decisive.  They average $0.52$ on food and $0.60$ on
exports, and they vary widely across the calendar, reaching one in three
months of the food series and four of the export series and zero in
three and two respectively.  A weight at one is the rule declaring that
the season's own residuals carry information the pooled standardised
buffer does not.  That the weights are dispersed rather than uniformly
high is what one should expect from twelve buffers of nineteen to
thirty-five observations: the rule is estimating a quantity it can only
see dimly, which is also why Experiment E6 found it no better than
fixing the weight at one half.

Two caveats bound how far any of this can be pushed.  The test period
contains $n_s = 10$ observations per season, so a per-season coverage is
a multiple of $0.1$ and a single month carries a standard error of about
$0.14$; the width comparisons, which rest on nineteen to thirty-five
training residuals per season, are considerably more reliable than the
coverage ones.  And the permutation test uses leave-one-out residuals
from a single fitted ensemble, so it inherits whatever misspecification
the Ridge predictor carries.
